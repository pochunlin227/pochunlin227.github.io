01 · Booking App · The Gap

One trip, three stages — coverage ended at stage one.

✓ COVERED

Pre-trip — search, book

Desktop booking worked well. This was the product's comfort zone.

✕ NOTHING

During trip — navigate, track

No product in the traveler's pocket. Trip details lived in email threads.

✕ NOTHING

Post-trip — reconcile, report

Receipts in camera rolls, spreadsheets and portals — every expense crossed five disconnected tools.

The easy read was "we need an app." Research said: the real issue is a broken post-booking workflow.

"I lost my invoice, so I cannot get reimbursed."— Business traveler, user interview

01 · Booking App · The Bet

Choosing what not to build.

Rejected

Desktop parity on mobile

  • 12-month build before any user value
  • Recreates features nobody needs mid-trip
  • High engineering & partner risk
Shipped

A lightweight mobile layer

  • One critical job: capture & organize expenses in-trip
  • Hybrid wrapper + native expense capture
  • Ships in one quarter — validates in weeks, not quarters

Flow decision: capture now, review later — nobody reviews a receipt while standing at the counter.

The decision logicHybrid bought speed; native expense capture bought credibility exactly where interaction quality mattered most.

02 · Promoted Users · Measure First

Measure before you move.

Growth work fails when you can't tell your effect from the noise. Before designing a single screen, we ran a one-month A/A test on the booking flow's optional questions — quantifying how many users were naturally business-inclined with zero intervention.

We believe a business entry card on the confirmation page will lift B4B registrations by ≥1%, because business travelers carry high decision costs — a clear signal tells them they've been seen. Guardrails that must not move: B2C booking rate · page load speed
Why this mattersWhen the variant won, nobody argued — everyone had agreed on the rules before the game started.

02 · Promoted Users · Ship & Test

Ship where the moment lives.

Three candidate channels — modal, email, confirmation page. The confirmation page won: every user passes through it, right when "travelling for work" is freshest. Validated with 8 usability sessions + 58 first-click tests before launch.

04 · Dyson · Reframe & Constraints

Reframing the brief, not the product.

What was briefed

A page-shaped problem, scoped as a content layout exercise.

What I reframed it as

"How do owners enter self-serve — and where are we losing them?"

A behaviour-shaped problem, solvable inside platform constraints by re-organising existing modules and copy.

Can't

Build new functionality on M2

No new backend, no new components.

Can

Reuse existing support pages

The win was the connective tissue — not new pages.

04 · Dyson · Three Models

Owners don't think in services. They think in products.

Three IA models, each betting on a different mental model: Service-first, Product-first by range, Product-first by SKU. Testing was unambiguous — SKU-level scored highest (SUS 84.75, 9/10 task completion) but demanded a heavy platform build.

The call: the lightest model unlocked the heaviest one.

The decision logicShip Service-first now — buildable within constraints. Make SKU-level the tested, evidence-backed north star for the platform's next generation.

05 · donateNYC · Research → Decisions

The research kept pointing at registration.

FINDING 1

User data wasn't clean

Duplicated locations and org info everywhere — undermining the city's reporting.

FINDING 2

One business, one login

Entire organizations shared a single account across dozens of store locations.

FINDING 3

The form was too long

Everything demanded up front — we lost users mid-registration.

Decision 1

Two-step sign-up

Step one: only what an admin needs to approve. Step two, at first login: service-specific questions. Shorter form, more completions, cleaner data.

Decision 2

Enterprise accounts

Six stakeholder types simplified to three roles; each business holds branches, locations, managers and staff — notifications reach the right person at the right store.

05 · donateNYC · Shipped for the City

Give goods. Find goods. Do good.

Beyond the registration fix: a directory map with filters, a food-matching workflow with a countdown so 25 recipients can respond before food expires, integrated messaging for auditable transactions, and scheduling for pick-ups.

06 · Arimo · Validation

Let riders vote with their fingers.

Hi-fi only where testing needed it. First-click heatmaps on the payment step confirmed the three-method layout; the 8-rider survey split exactly as the research predicted — new-city riders want a fast default, home-city riders want their plans. One flow had to serve both.

07 · Beacon · The Map System

Your day, drawn as a journey.

"Today's Schedule" renders as a route: events are pins, travel legs carry duration and mode, past events dim. Time Slots budget the prep chain — shower 15, breakfast 15, commute 40 — so "leave by 8:05" is arithmetic, not guesswork.

6modules, all upstream of one trustworthy depart alert
2zoom levels with meaning: next hour vs the whole day
The reframeA schedule isn't a list of times — it's a route through a city. Calendars managed time; the minutes between events were nobody's job.

07 · Beacon · Epilogue

The instinct aged well.

Depart-time alerts, ETA sharing, voice event capture, calendar-map integrations — mainstream products shipped every core idea here in the years after 2017.

What held up

Geography is the context that makes time useful

The direction was right — and it became how I think about every Geo problem since.

What I'd redo

One hero moment, not six modules

Users said routine setup was annoying; we built a setup-heavy builder anyway. Today: learned patterns over manual config, and ship the depart alert first.

Why it closes the deckEvery project since — Booking's trip timelines, Dyson's support journeys — asks the same question at different scales: where is the user, where are they going, what do they need to get there?
