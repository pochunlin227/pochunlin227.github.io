Design exercise · Survey pre-registration

What I will do with the data before I have it

Fifty responses are in the field. They may not land before this is due — so the predictions and the decisions they trigger are written down first, while they can still be wrong.

← Overview · the brief

Why write this first Method

A prediction made after seeing the data is not a prediction. Committing to the decision rule up front is the only thing that stops a survey from becoming decoration for choices already made.

It also protects against the more likely outcome here, which is that the numbers arrive late or thin. If the design decision is already tied to a threshold, late data still has a job to do — and if the data never comes, the reasoning stands on its own.

What this instrument can and cannot settle Limits

  • Roughly 30–40 usable responses. Fifty in the field, minus those screened out at Q1. Directional only, and nowhere near enough to cut by segment — six motivations across thirty-five people is five per cell, which is a story, not a finding.
  • Stated privacy concern will be inflated. Q5 and Q15 ask how worried people are, and privacy is the textbook case where stated and revealed preference diverge. My own drop-off is the counter-example: I did not leave over privacy, I left because I did not understand a word.
  • Q4 and Q11 are stated intention. Weak predictors of behaviour at the best of times, and Q11 asks people to rate my screen inside my own survey — expect a flattering number. The open text at Q12 and Q13 is where the signal actually is.
  • Q9 and Q10 are the only behavioural anchor. Recalled action, not imagined action. They get weighted above everything else.

Variants drawn in advance So the data has somewhere to land

Each of these is a screen I would rather have ready than start after the results arrive.

  • Time-framing pair. One version states a total up front, as the current screen does. The other shows no total, only the step you are on and what is left, plus “you can stop and resume.” Q17 picks the winner.
  • Regulation-forward header. The sheet led by licensing rather than by benefit. Held in reserve against Q7 surprising me.
  • The failure path. What a person sees when the check does not pass. The screen currently promises a survivable failure; if Q16 ranks it highly, that promise needs designing rather than asserting.

What I would fix in the instrument If it is not too late

  • Randomise option order in Q5, Q6 and Q7. Order effects in select-up-to-three lists are large enough to invent a winner.
  • Put Q12 and Q13 before Q11. Rating the screen first anchors the open text that follows, and the open text is the part worth having.
  • Add an escape option to Q7 — “none of these would change how I feel.” Without it, a forced choice manufactures a preference.
  • Add one question after Q10: did you ever come back and finish? That single answer decides whether the save-and-resume exit is worth building, and nothing else in the survey asks it.
