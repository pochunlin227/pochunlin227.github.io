The survey, and where I was wrong

100 responses across three markets, scored against predictions written down before the data arrived. Three of six were wrong, and one of them changes what the screen should lead with.

The headline n = 100, three markets

Only 34% would continue straight away. 42% would go looking for a reason first. Hesitation, not refusal, is what this screen is up against.

Fielded via Pollfish, 100 completes across the USA, Australia and Singapore. Respondents were not screened out by crypto experience: 43% had bought crypto before, 19% had considered it, 38% never had — and half the sample was not at all or only slightly familiar with the category.

The single most useful number. Two-thirds were not ready to move forward immediately: 42% would read more about why verification is required, 9% would leave, 7% would pause, 8% were unsure. Almost nobody rejects the ask outright. They stall in front of it.

So the path preview is not disproven as an aid to finishing; it is disproven as a reason to believe. The honest conclusion is that the screen was weighted toward the wrong job.

The screen itself Q18–Q20 concept test

Shown the design, 51% said they would be likely or very likely to continue, 28% were neutral and 21% unlikely. I had predicted a flattering number; 21% negative is not flattering, and the neutral fifth is the real prize.

Asked what one thing they would change, 60% of coded suggestions were about data protection and privacy (51 of 85 substantive answers); 31.8% said nothing needed changing. Recurring verbatims asked for the same few things: where the ID is stored, how long it is kept, who sees it, and proof the platform is legitimate — “More credentials visible,” “I don't really see any logos,” “which governmental agency it uses to verify,” “The ID information will be deleted once it's been verified.”

Two criticisms landed on the craft rather than the content, and both were repeated: the screen carries too much at once — “Too much information,” “a lot of information to process,” “the whole lot looks confusing” — and it is hard to read — “Bigger font,” “some of the text colour makes it difficult to read,” “use more professional colours and make it seem more secure.”

What this changes Decisions, now grounded

  • Lead with data protection, not with theft protection. The encryption message won the forced choice (31%), data protection is the most important information on the screen (59% extremely important), and 60% of requested changes are about privacy. The current headline — so nobody else can buy in your name — is the runner-up framing.
  • Say what happens to the document, not just that it is encrypted. Where it is stored, how long it is kept, who can see it, when it is deleted. Respondents asked for this unprompted and repeatedly.
  • Put licensing back on the screen. Regulatory information ties for second among trust cues (3.8). Cutting it was the call I flagged as worth checking, and the check says I was wrong.
  • Demote the time estimate. Least persuasive message (6%) and least important information (19%). It should not occupy a prominent pill. Tolerance is not the issue — only 7% would bail under a minute.
  • Keep “nothing charged yet.” Only 5% name it as a stop reason, but 43% are extremely concerned about it when asked and 45% call the confirmation extremely important. Cheap to keep, expensive to remove.
  • Shrink the path preview; do not delete it. Last among trust cues, but it answers a real question for the 15% segment that wants rationale before acting. It earns a line, not a card.
  • Make it visibly institutional. Bigger type, higher contrast, credential marks. “More professional colours and make it seem more secure” is a direct vote for the light, bank-like register over the neon one.

What this still cannot settle Limits

  • No screener. 38% had never considered buying crypto, and they answered a first-time-buyer scenario anyway. Their hesitation is about crypto, not about this screen. The real target — someone mid-purchase — is a subset I cannot isolate here.
  • Stated, not observed. Everything except Q16 is a hypothetical. 51% saying they would continue is not 51% continuing.
  • Markets differ more than the average suggests. Singapore: 70% would read more first. Australia: 30% would leave. USA: an even split. The sample is too small to treat these as findings, but too divergent to assume one screen converts everywhere.
  • The screen was judged as an image. Nobody tapped anything. Every claim about the sheet, the method chooser and the flow is untested.

What I would run next. Instrument the live funnel and compare a data-protection-led variant against the current benefit-led one, with completion within 24 hours as the primary measure. That is the one question this survey raised and cannot answer.
