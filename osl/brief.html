The reframe What the screen is for

The screen's job is not to justify the identity check. It is to be the last unknown in the flow.

Verification is non-negotiable — customer due diligence is a standing expectation for virtual asset service providers. How predictable it feels is ours. “First-time buyer” is also not one person: six motivations arrive here, each with its own objection, and the screen has to answer all six without naming any.

Design decisions Why this screen

  • Introduce the stranger first. The buyer tapped Buy inside another app; a company they have never heard of now wants a passport. One line names the handoff — and that this is the name on their statement. Built for two entry contexts: a self-custody wallet and a consumer money app.
  • Prompt in place before interrupting. Verification arrives as a banner inside the buy screen, beside the live price and the amount they chose. A hard interruption is the moment a first-time buyer leaves and does not come back. The banner cannot be dismissed — the step is real — but it never seizes the screen either.
  • Buy stays visible, and stays off. The purchase button is disabled until the check is done, but never leaves: the destination stays in view, and the banner directly above it is the way through. A gated primary action needs its reason within a thumb's reach or it reads as a bug.
  • The reason is the headline, not a footnote. The banner and the sheet both lead with the benefit — so nobody else can buy in your name — and the privacy answer sits in the same breath, because “never sold” is the specific worry people voice about ID uploads. The regulatory reason is off the surface: it explains our obligation, not theirs. It was the first thing I cut, and whether that was right is worth checking.
  • Show the whole remaining path. Four steps, total time, what comes next. Unpredictability, not identity, is what makes this feel punitive — and this makes “necessary, not punitive” checkable rather than asserted. The wallet address, from a real drop-off, is answered one line before they meet it.
  • Offer the method, not just the mandate. The sheet goes straight to how — camera, upload, or tapping a passport to the phone. The live question is what your hands will do. The chip option is there because it is fastest and most flows omit it.
  • Close the loop out loud. On success the banner is replaced by a verified row, Buy switches on, and the sheet says the order survived and the price is live again — answering, at the only moment it can be proved, the fear that started the whole hesitation.
  • Make the exit useful, and refuse punitive tone. No Cancel; the only way out saves the order and sends a link. No red, no Required badge, no padlock as a barrier, and the failure path described before anyone fails.

Research approach Why people leave here

Three populations sit behind one drop-off number and need opposite fixes: people who won't (don't accept or trust the ask), who can't (no ID to hand, unsupported document, name mismatch) and who got stuck (capture failure, rejection, silent pending). Sizing them precedes any copy decision.

  • Instrument the step, not the screen. Every sub-event from view to paid, with retries and back taps, cut by partner, document type and device — separating never-started from started-and-broke.
  • Ask at the moment of leaving. One question on back-out — the only instrument that reaches people who never return.
  • Mine what exists. Tickets, chat transcripts and store reviews, coded for theme — a day's work that usually reframes the problem before a session is booked.
  • Watch first-timers for real — own phone, at home, from a partner app. “My passport is upstairs” never happens in a lab.
  • Call abandoners within 48 hours, while the decision is recalled rather than reconstructed.
  • Then experiment, one lever per arm, so results attribute to a cause.

The assumption I'd validate What this bets

First-time buyers do not necessarily abandon because verification exists. They abandon because it appears unexpectedly, and they cannot predict its purpose, its effort, its privacy implications, or whether the purchase they already committed to will survive it.

Where it comes from. Baymard puts documented checkout abandonment at 70.22% across fifty studies — distrust over card details 19%, forced account creation 18%, checkout too long or complicated 17%. Unexpected demands, not price. The sharper input is my own drop-off, because it was not the identity check: I cleared an on-ramp's verification and stopped at Your wallet address, did not know what one was, and left.

How I'd test it. Code exit-survey text against the four predictability gaps — purpose, effort, privacy, order safety — plus a fifth for plain capability, and size them. Then two arms: one changes only the reasons and tone, the other only predictability. If the concept is right, the predictability arm carries the lift.

What would prove me wrong. If can't and stuck together exceed roughly half the drop-off, or the reasons arm wins, this screen is decoration and the work belongs downstream in capture, ID coverage and resume flows. The banner carries its own risk worth measuring separately: it is persistent but non-blocking, which protects the session and can also be scrolled straight past, so I would watch banner-to-sheet rate against a blocking variant before assuming gentler is better.

AI in my process Where it helped

Used for. Breadth early — twenty framings of the reasons block so I could see the range rather than take the first; a desk scan of how regulated apps word this step; first-pass coding of tickets and open text, hand-checked on a sample; and building the prototypes fast.

Not trusted with. Anything standing in for a real person — it has never had a passport upstairs or stalled on a word it did not know. Not why a number moved. Not final regulatory or privacy wording, which is compliance's. And never real user data into a general model.

Asked for — explain “wallet address” in one sentence

A wallet address is a unique alphanumeric identifier that represents the destination of your cryptocurrency transaction on the blockchain. Fluent, accurate, useless at the moment it is needed.

Rejected — what shipped instead

Where your Bitcoin gets delivered — like an account number, for crypto. MetaMask already gave us yours. Written after sitting with what the question actually is.

Verdict: rejected. Someone asking what a wallet address is wants to know do I have one, and am I about to lose my money getting this wrong. The unblocking sentence is “you already have one, and we have it” — a fact that lives in the partner integration, not in the language.
