The product thesis What the screen is for

KYC is required. Surprise is not.

For a first-time buyer, identity verification can feel like the product changes halfway through: they choose an amount and payment method, then an unfamiliar provider asks for an ID.

The survey says the barrier is hesitation rather than refusal: only 34% would continue straight away, 42% would go looking for a reason first, and just 9% would leave. So the screen's job is to answer the question people stall on — who is handling this, why it matters to me, and what happens to my data and my order — before it asks for anything sensitive.

100 survey responses 10+ competitor flows reviewed OSL design language

Design process Three inputs

  • First-time buyer survey. 100 responses across the USA, Australia and Singapore on how people react when KYC appears after they have chosen an amount and payment method. It tested trust, privacy, payment safety, effort, message framing, and the screen itself.
  • Competitor review. More than 10 crypto buying and payment apps, compared on when KYC appears, how the request is explained, whether order and payment status stay visible, how methods are presented, and what happens when users meet unfamiliar terms.
  • OSL design language. Colour, typography, hierarchy, and interaction patterns come from OSL. This keeps verification inside the OSL ecosystem rather than feeling like a handoff to a stranger.

Design decisions Why this screen

  • Keep the purchase in view. The amount, payment method, and Buy action stay visible. Verification becomes the path to completing the purchase, not a compliance flow that interrupts it.
  • Lead with the user benefit — and the survey says which one. Buyer protection, privacy and order safety come before regulatory detail, but data protection outranks theft protection: the encryption message won the forced choice (31% vs 27%), 59% call data handling the most important thing to know, and 60% of requested changes were about privacy. The screen currently leads with theft protection, which is the runner-up. Licensing also belongs back on the surface — it ties second among trust cues.
  • Make the next step feel finite — quietly. What is required, which methods are available, and what happens after approval. The time estimate stays but loses its prominence: it was the least persuasive message tested (6%) and the least important information (19%), and only 7% would abandon under a minute. Predictability helps people finish; it does not make them trust you.
  • Prepare users before they meet unfamiliar terms. Someone can be willing to verify and still abandon later at a term like “wallet address.” Concepts belong at the point of need, not after the user is already blocked.
  • Use visual continuity to build trust. No warning-heavy patterns, no alarm colour. Verification is treated as a normal part of a financial journey.

The first survey helps identify perceived concerns. The next phase would measure where users actually leave.

  • Instrument the entire journey. Track progression, retries, back-outs, completion time, and failure states by partner, device, market, and document type.
  • Separate the drop-off into three groups. Won't proceed — users who do not trust or accept the request. Can't proceed — users with document, eligibility, or access issues. Get stuck — users who begin but fail during capture, review, or recovery.
  • Ask at the point of exit. One back-out question — “What stopped you today?” — reaches people who never return.
  • Pair data with real behaviour. Support tickets, chat themes, and app-store feedback first. Then watch first-time buyers on their own phones, at home, where the real friction shows up.
  • Test one lever at a time. Reassurance, information timing, and verification mechanics move separately, so any lift can be attributed to a cause.

What the survey settled, and what it didn't Limits

  • Hesitation is the commercial risk, not rejection. 34% would continue immediately, 42% want a reason first, 9% would leave. The middle group is the one worth designing for.
  • Shown the screen, 51% would likely continue, 28% were neutral, 21% unlikely. Credible, not yet persuasive — and the neutral fifth is where the gain is.
  • No screener. 38% had never considered buying crypto and answered a first-time-buyer scenario anyway. Their hesitation is about the category, not this screen.
  • Stated, not observed. Everything but the recalled-abandonment question is hypothetical, and the screen was judged as a static image — nobody tapped anything, so the sheet and the method chooser are untested.
  • Markets diverge more than the average admits. Singapore 70% would read more first; Australia 30% would leave; the USA splits evenly. Too small to be a finding, too wide to ignore.

The assumption to validate What this design bets

First-time buyers are not one abandonment group. Some leave because an unfamiliar provider asking for ID creates a trust and privacy concern. Others are willing to complete KYC, but abandon when crypto-specific concepts — such as a wallet address — appear without enough explanation at the moment they need to act.

This treats abandonment as both a trust gap and a comprehension gap.

The survey answered this, and it was not close. Trust dominates: worried data could be misused 48%, uneasy sharing an ID or selfie 44%, unsure the platform can be trusted 32%. Comprehension is real but smaller — 25% do not understand why verification is needed. Effort barely registers: how long 10%, what documents 5%, already charged 5%. So the work is provider transparency, privacy reassurance and visible legitimacy first; contextual explanation second.

The kill criterion did not fire. Capability and operational failure are almost absent from the recalled-abandonment verbatims — one respondent cited a crash; the rest describe a feeling of sketchiness. This screen is the right surface. I had predicted the opposite, which is recorded in full on the results page.

AI in my process Where it helped

AI was used as an exploration and synthesis assistant — not as the source of product truth.

What I used it for. Building an early map of the crypto buying journey and common first-time buyer questions; summarising publicly available crypto, payment, and checkout research to identify themes worth investigating; drafting the initial survey plan and alternative question wording; creating a structured framework for reviewing 10+ competitor purchase flows; exploring different KYC messaging and information-hierarchy options; and building and iterating the HTML prototypes quickly.

How I evaluated it. I compared an AI-first output, made with minimal direction, against a version shaped by my own research, competitor review, and survey design. The AI-first version ran to generic reassurance copy and too much at once. I used it to see the range, then refined against the real task, the partner context, and the evidence.

What I would not trust it with. Replacing user research or deciding which user concern matters most; explaining why a conversion metric changed without behavioural evidence; final compliance, privacy, or regulatory wording; and processing real user identities, documents, tickets, or other sensitive data in a general-purpose model.

A wallet address is a unique alphanumeric identifier that represents the destination of your cryptocurrency transaction on the blockchain. Technically correct, and it answers a question nobody asked.

This is where your crypto will be delivered — like an account number for crypto. If you are buying through your wallet, it may already be connected for you. Final wording still to be verified against the actual partner integration.

Verdict: modified. The original does not answer the first-time buyer's real question — do I already have one, and could I lose my money if I get this wrong?
