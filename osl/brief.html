The product thesis What the screen is for

KYC is required. Surprise is not.

For a first-time buyer, identity verification can feel like the product changes halfway through: they choose an amount and payment method, then an unfamiliar provider asks for an ID.

This screen is designed to make that required step feel predictable rather than punitive. Before asking for anything sensitive, it clarifies who is handling verification, why it matters to the buyer, what they need to do, and what happens to the order while they do it.

~50 survey responses 10+ competitor flows reviewed OSL design language

Design process Three inputs

  • First-time buyer survey. I ran an early survey with approximately 50 participants to understand how people react when KYC appears after they have selected an amount and payment method. It explored trust, privacy, payment safety, effort, and unfamiliar crypto terminology.
  • Competitor review. I reviewed more than 10 crypto buying and payment apps to understand how identity verification is introduced in real purchase flows — when KYC appears, how providers explain the request, whether order and payment status remain visible, how verification methods are presented, and how users are supported when they meet unfamiliar terms.
  • OSL design language. The interface uses OSL's design language as its base: colour, typography, hierarchy, and interaction patterns. This keeps the verification moment connected to the OSL ecosystem, rather than making it feel like a suspicious or disconnected third-party handoff.

Design decisions Why this screen

  • Keep the purchase in view. The selected amount, payment method, and Buy action remain visible. Verification is positioned as the path to completing the purchase — not a separate compliance flow that interrupts it.
  • Lead with the user benefit. The screen explains verification through buyer protection, privacy, and order safety before introducing regulatory detail. At this moment users need to know why an ID request protects them, not only why the business is required to make it.
  • Make the next step feel finite. The flow shows what is required, expected time, available verification methods, and what happens after approval. This reduces the feeling of entering an open-ended process.
  • Prepare users before they meet unfamiliar terms. A first-time buyer may be willing to verify their identity but still abandon later when they see a term such as “wallet address” without context. Important concepts should be explained at the point of need, not after the user is already blocked.
  • Use visual continuity to build trust. OSL's visual language provides a familiar, calm foundation for a sensitive moment. The design avoids warning-heavy patterns and treats verification as a normal part of a financial journey.

The first survey helps identify perceived concerns. The next phase would measure where users actually leave.

  • Instrument the entire journey. Track progression, retries, back-outs, completion time, and failure states by partner, device, market, and document type.
  • Separate the drop-off into three groups. Won't proceed — users who do not trust or accept the request. Can't proceed — users with document, eligibility, or access issues. Get stuck — users who begin but fail during capture, review, or recovery.
  • Ask at the point of exit. A lightweight back-out question — “What stopped you today?” — reaches users who may never return.
  • Pair data with real behaviour. Review support tickets, chat themes, and app-store feedback. Then observe first-time buyers on their own devices and in their own environment, where contextual friction is more visible.
  • Test one lever at a time. Test reassurance, information timing, and verification mechanics separately, so any improvement in completion can be attributed to a clear cause.

What this instrument can and cannot settle Limits

  • Roughly 30–40 usable responses. Fifty in the field, minus those screened out at Q1. Directional only, and nowhere near enough to cut by segment — six motivations across thirty-five people is five per cell, which is a story, not a finding.
  • Stated privacy concern will be inflated. Q5 and Q15 ask how worried people are, and privacy is the textbook case where stated and revealed preference diverge. My own drop-off is the counter-example: I did not leave over privacy, I left because I did not understand a word.
  • Q4 and Q11 are stated intention. Weak predictors of behaviour at the best of times, and Q11 asks people to rate my screen inside my own survey — expect a flattering number. The open text at Q12 and Q13 is where the signal actually is.
  • Q9 and Q10 are the only behavioural anchor. Recalled action, not imagined action. They get weighted above everything else.

The assumption to validate What this design bets

First-time buyers are not one abandonment group. Some leave because an unfamiliar provider asking for ID creates a trust and privacy concern. Others are willing to complete KYC, but abandon when crypto-specific concepts — such as a wallet address — appear without enough explanation at the moment they need to act.

This treats abandonment as both a trust gap and a comprehension gap.

If trust-related concerns dominate, the product should improve provider transparency, privacy reassurance, and payment clarity. If comprehension-related concerns dominate, the product should introduce contextual explanations and improve handoffs throughout the purchase journey.

If document, capture, or review failures account for most drop-off, this screen is not the main problem. The work should move downstream into ID coverage, capture quality, review states, and recovery flows.

AI in my process Where it helped

AI was used as an exploration and synthesis assistant — not as the source of product truth.

What I used it for. Building an early map of the crypto buying journey and common first-time buyer questions; summarising publicly available crypto, payment, and checkout research to identify themes worth investigating; drafting the initial survey plan and alternative question wording; creating a structured framework for reviewing 10+ competitor purchase flows; exploring different KYC messaging and information-hierarchy options; and building and iterating the HTML prototypes quickly.

How I evaluated it. I compared an AI-first output, created with minimal direction, with a version shaped through my own research, competitor review, survey design, and product reasoning. The AI-first output usually produced more generic reassurance copy and too much information at once. I used it to explore the range, then reviewed and refined against the actual user task, partner context, and evidence collected.

What I would not trust it with. Replacing user research or deciding which user concern matters most; explaining why a conversion metric changed without behavioural evidence; final compliance, privacy, or regulatory wording; and processing real user identities, documents, tickets, or other sensitive data in a general-purpose model.
