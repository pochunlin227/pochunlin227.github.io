Introducing identity verification without losing the buyer

A single screen that asks a first-time crypto buyer for their ID at the moment they are ready to pay. This is the thinking behind it: the decisions on the screen, how I researched the drop-off, the assumption I put in front of users, and where AI belonged.

The problem What the screen is for

Nobody refuses the check. They stall in front of it — because they cannot see what happens to their ID.

The moment. A first-time buyer has chosen an amount and a payment method. Then a company they have never heard of asks for a passport and a selfie.

What 100 people said. Only 34% would continue straight away. 42% would go looking for a reason first, and 9% would leave. What stops them is exposure, not effort — 48% fear their data being misused, 44% are uneasy sharing an ID, 32% doubt the platform. Not knowing how long it takes: 10%.

The bet. Lead with what happens to the document — encrypted, who can see it, when it is deleted — and show the licence standing behind it. Everything else on the screen is secondary to that.

How I ran it Frame, build, test, revise

Design and research ran together rather than in sequence. That is why the survey could test a real screen instead of a description, and why the revision had something specific to revise.

Three inputs fed it.

  • First-time buyer survey. 100 responses across the USA, Australia and Singapore on how people react when KYC appears after they have chosen an amount and payment method. It tested trust, privacy, payment safety, effort, message framing, and the screen itself.
  • Competitor review. More than 10 crypto buying and payment apps, compared on when KYC appears, how the request is explained, whether order and payment status stay visible, how methods are presented, and what happens when users meet unfamiliar terms.
  • OSL design language. Colour, typography and hierarchy come from OSL; the interaction patterns come from the flows above. This keeps verification inside the OSL ecosystem rather than feeling like a handoff to a stranger.

Design decisions Why this screen

  • Let users keep seeing what they are buying. I kept the amount, payment method, and Buy button on the screen. That way, verification feels like one necessary step to complete their purchase, rather than being suddenly taken into a separate compliance process.
  • Start with what users care about most: their personal data. From the survey, users cared most about how their information would be handled. So instead of leading with regulations, the screen first reassures them that their data is protected and encrypted. Licensing and security are still shown, but in a more supportive role.
  • Make verification feel clear and manageable. I wanted users to understand what they need to do, what verification options they have, and what will happen after they are approved. I removed the exact time estimate because the research showed that speed alone does not build much trust. For first-time buyers, knowing what to expect matters more.
  • Explain unfamiliar crypto terms before they become a problem. As someone new to crypto, I might be ready to verify my identity but still get confused later by terms like “wallet address.” I would explain these concepts at the moment users need them, instead of waiting until they are already stuck.
  • Make verification feel like a normal part of buying, not a warning. I avoided scary language, warning colours, or a design that feels like the user has done something wrong. The visual style stays consistent with the purchase journey, so KYC feels like a normal safety step — similar to what users may already expect from banking or other financial services.

How I would research abandonment What people do, not what they say

The survey helps me understand what first-time buyers say they worry about. But to understand abandonment properly, I would next look at what people actually do in the real verification journey.

First, I would map where users drop off.

KYC prompt→ Start verification→ Choose a method→ Take or upload documents→ Submit→ Get approved→ Complete purchase

I would track how many users move from one step to the next, where they leave, retry, go back, or encounter an error. I would also compare this across device type, market, partner, and document type.

Then, I would group the reasons behind drop-off into three simple categories:

  • “I don't want to do this.” The user does not understand why verification is needed, does not trust the company, or feels surprised by the request.
  • “I can't do this right now.” The user does not have the right document, is not eligible, or cannot access what they need.
  • “I tried, but got stuck.” The user starts verification but struggles with taking a photo, uploading a document, waiting for review, or recovering from an error.

This distinction is important because each problem needs a different design solution. More reassurance may help someone who does not trust us, but it will not help someone whose document upload keeps failing.

I would also ask users when they leave. For users who exit the flow, I would show one short optional question, such as: “What stopped you from verifying today?” This helps us learn from people who may never come back or contact support.

I would combine the data with real user behaviour. I would review support tickets, live-chat themes, and app-store feedback to identify repeated issues. Then I would run usability sessions with first-time crypto buyers on their own phones, in their own environment. This is especially useful for KYC because camera access, document handling, lighting, and unfamiliar financial terms can all create friction that is hard to see in a prototype.

Finally, I would test improvements one at a time. For example, I would separately test:

  • clearer privacy and data-handling reassurance
  • explaining why KYC is needed earlier or later
  • different verification methods
  • better document-capture guidance and error recovery

This lets me understand why conversion improves, rather than changing everything at once and guessing.

I would judge each test on verification completed within 24 hours of first seeing the screen — and hold it against fraud and chargeback rate, because a friction win that lets bad actors through is not a win.

What the survey answered And what it still cannot

The survey gave me useful direction, but I would not treat it as the final answer.

  • The biggest opportunity is not convincing people who strongly reject KYC. It is helping the large middle group: 42% said they would want an explanation before continuing.
  • When users saw the screen, 51% said they would likely continue, while 28% were neutral. That tells me the concept is credible, but there is still room to make it feel more reassuring and clear.
  • However, some survey participants had never considered buying crypto before. Their hesitation may be about crypto in general, not specifically about this KYC screen.
  • Most answers were based on a static design image and hypothetical questions. We still need to test the actual interaction: opening the information, choosing a verification method, uploading a document, and recovering from errors.
  • The country-level responses also varied quite a lot. The sample is too small to make strong market decisions yet, but it is a signal that I would validate this in key markets rather than assuming one message works everywhere.

The assumption to validate What this design bets

First-time buyers are not one abandonment group. Some leave because an unfamiliar provider asking for ID creates a trust and privacy concern. Others are willing to complete KYC, but abandon when crypto-specific concepts — such as a wallet address — appear without enough explanation at the moment they need to act.

This treats abandonment as both a trust gap and a comprehension gap.

The survey answered this, and it was not close. Trust dominates: worried data could be misused 48%, uneasy sharing an ID or selfie 44%, unsure the platform can be trusted 32%. Comprehension is real but smaller — 25% do not understand why verification is needed. Effort barely registers: how long 10%, what documents 5%, already charged 5%. So the work is provider transparency, privacy reassurance and visible legitimacy first; contextual explanation second.

It also told me this screen is the right surface to work on. Capability and operational failure are almost absent from the recalled-abandonment answers — one respondent cited a crash; the rest describe a feeling of sketchiness. I had expected the opposite, and would have moved the work downstream into document capture and error recovery had it gone the other way. The full results are here.

AI in my process Where it helped

AI was used as an exploration and synthesis assistant — not as the source of product truth.

What I used it for. Building an early map of the crypto buying journey and common first-time buyer questions; summarising publicly available crypto, payment, and checkout research to identify themes worth investigating; drafting the initial survey plan and alternative question wording; creating a structured framework for reviewing 10+ competitor purchase flows; exploring different KYC messaging and information-hierarchy options; and building and iterating the HTML prototypes quickly.

How I evaluated it. I compared an AI-first output, made with minimal direction, against a version shaped by my own research, competitor review, and survey design. The AI-first version ran to generic reassurance copy and too much at once. I used it to see the range, then refined against the real task, the partner context, and the evidence.

What I would not trust it with. Replacing user research or deciding which user concern matters most; explaining why a conversion metric changed without behavioural evidence; final compliance, privacy, or regulatory wording; and processing real user identities, documents, tickets, or other sensitive data in a general-purpose model.
